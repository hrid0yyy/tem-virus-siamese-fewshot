Dear Editors,

I am pleased to submit the original research article \textbf{``Generalised few-shot identification of rare viruses in negative-stain transmission electron microscopy images: a controlled comparison of metric-learning and classification features''} for consideration in \textit{Array}. The manuscript was previously submitted to \textit{Micron}, whose editors suggested \textit{Array} as a more suitable journal.

\textbf{Why this journal.} The study addresses a core machine-learning problem, generalised few-shot recognition, in which new classes must be recognised from a handful of examples alongside the classes seen in training. It applies metric learning to a real computer-vision task, the identification of viruses in negative-stain transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images, where rare and newly emerging viruses have very few reference images. The emphasis on a fair comparison with a strong baseline, validation-only model selection, a duplicate audit and fully released code fits the scope of \textit{Array} in machine learning, computer vision and medical informatics.

\textbf{What is known.} The public TEM virus dataset of Matuszewski and Sintorn (2021) contains negative-stain micrographs of 22 viruses, but its benchmark covers only the 14 most frequent. The eight rare viruses, with 5--25 training micrographs each, have been excluded, pooled into closed-set classifiers trained on every class, or used only as unknown samples for out-of-distribution detection; as far as I could determine, no study has evaluated them as viruses recognised from a few examples on the official split.

\textbf{What this study adds.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=0pt]
\item \textbf{A controlled generalised few-shot benchmark.} Encoders trained on the 14 frequent viruses are applied to the eight rare viruses, which they never saw in training and which are added from their reference images alone. A Siamese (supervised contrastive) encoder is compared with a faithful re-implementation of the benchmark classifier under identical evaluation; on the 14 frequent viruses the two perform on par (\SiamClosedAcc\,\% versus \BOneClosedAcc\,\% accuracy, three seeds each).
\item \textbf{What drives rare-virus recognition.} With a single reference particle per rare virus and nearest-prototype classification, the Siamese features recognise rare viruses better (\KshotSiamOne\,\% versus \KshotBOneOne\,\% rare-class accuracy), but this edge vanishes with a stronger one-shot classifier or more examples. The classifier fitted on frozen features matters more than the training recipe.
\item \textbf{Which viruses are confused, and why.} Errors occur mainly between viruses that look alike in negative-stain TEM: among the arenaviruses (Guanarito, LCM, Machupo and Lassa virus), between the parapoxviruses Pseudocowpox and Orf, and between small, near-spherical particles such as Dengue virus and Sapovirus. These pairs indicate where expert confirmation and additional reference images are most needed.
\item \textbf{A simple remedy.} A linear classifier on the frozen features raises the 22-class macro-F1 from \SiamNcmFOne\ to \SiamLrFOne\ without retraining, although Dengue virus remains difficult.
\item \textbf{Open and transparent.} Negative results are reported in full, and the code, trained models and per-patch predictions are publicly available.
\end{itemize}
